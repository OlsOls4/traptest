# How to evaluate a tx-intelligence provider

Telegraph ranks AI providers by intelligence. A tx-intelligence provider claims to know what happened on-chain: give it a transaction hash, and it tells you the status, sender, recipient, value and block. The claim is easy to make and hard to check — unless you already know the answer before you ask. That is the whole method: buy a question whose ground truth is known in advance, from competing providers, and score each answer pass or fail against that truth.

A trap is that question. It is a real transaction on Base Sepolia, picked because its record is already public and checkable. Before any provider is scored, the trap's ground truth — status, sender, recipient, value, block — is verified against the public Base Sepolia RPC, and the set is frozen. Freezing matters: once buying starts, the questions cannot change. A set tuned after seeing answers, to flatter one provider or punish another, would invalidate the run. TrapTest froze 12 traps on 6 November 2026 and 12 more on 9 November, across six categories: plain transfers, token transfers, contract interactions, reverted transactions, multi-recipient transactions, and unknown transactions — hashes with no on-chain record at all, which catch providers that invent details for something that does not exist.

Each trap is bought from each provider separately, through Telegraph's Direct Ask, at $0.01 per call in testnet USDC. Every served call returns a signal hash and a Telegraph receipt, and the receipt's settlement transaction is verifiable on Sepolia Etherscan. That receipt is the difference between a claim and evidence: it proves the question was actually bought and paid for. It does not prove the answer was right — that is what scoring is for.

Scoring is deliberately strict. Status must match exactly; a wrong status fails the trap outright, and a missing field is a failure, never a pass. Overall pass requires every scored field to match. The strictness is the point: a provider that returns four right fields and one wrong one has not answered the question correctly. But pass rate alone is not the whole story, so TrapTest reports field accuracy beside it — the share of individually scored fields that were right. The two numbers together say more than either alone. On the 12-trap expansion set, Truvian passed 2 of 12 traps but got 39 of 49 fields right; TxLens passed the same 2 of 12 but only 2 of 47 fields. Same pass rate, very different providers — one is a schema gap away from correct, the other is answering `not_found` for transactions that are mined and confirmed.

That distinction — schema gap versus wrong answer — is the most useful thing a trap run teaches. Truvian returns no `status` field at all for confirmed transactions, while its block, sender, recipient and value are all correct. Under strict scoring those traps still fail, and the failure is reported honestly as a gap, not as a lie. TxLens's `not_found` answers are wrong answers, not a gap: the transactions exist. ChainWire, the third provider on the expansion set, shows what a complete answer looks like: 11 of 12 traps passed and 46 of 47 fields right, with its single miss a value field on one reverted transaction.

Two findings from the runs are worth carrying into any evaluation you build. The first is about payment. Failed calls can still settle: on the frozen set, 24 calls to two providers that could not serve the intent still settled $0.01 each — $0.24 in total — into Telegraph escrow credit, not billed. The other provider's failures, returning 503s, settled nothing. If you reconcile a burner balance against billed cost and find a gap, check escrow before assuming an error. The second is about paying at all. The x402 v2 payment flow needs the credential in the `PAYMENT-SIGNATURE` header, not `X-PAYMENT` (which the server silently ignores and answers with another 402), with the v2 payload shape and the authorisation's numeric fields as strings — JSON numbers fail verification. That cost TrapTest a round of 402s before the exact shape was found; it is documented in the repo so the next builder does not pay it again.

To run your own evaluation, start by inspecting before spending: read the frozen trap files and the leaderboard, both free. Pick a transaction, verify its ground truth against the public RPC yourself, and write it as a one-trap file. Dry-run the loop first — it validates traps, key, providers and output path and spends nothing. Then replay one trap against two providers for $0.02 and check the receipts. If a provider fails, say which field failed and publish the receipt beside the score. A leaderboard with no failures is a weaker result than one that shows them; the failures are what prove the ranking can be trusted.
